The moratorium — read this twice

During your course (plus 6–12 months after), you pay no EMI. But interest keeps adding up on the full amount. On a ₹15 lakh loan at 9.5%, two years of moratorium adds roughly ₹3 lakh to what you owe before the first EMI. If you can, pay just the interest during the course — it keeps the loan from ballooning.

Good news: families with annual income under ₹4.5 lakh can claim the interest subsidy (CSIS) for the moratorium period on education loans for technical and professional courses in India. Ask your bank about it before you sign.

    Six things nobody explains

    Tap any card. Each one takes thirty seconds and can save you real money.

    How is an EMI actually calculated?›
    Your EMI comes from three numbers only: how much you borrow, the reducing-balance interest rate, and the number of months. Interest is charged each month on the balance that is still unpaid — so as you repay, the interest part of your EMI shrinks and the principal part grows. That is why the same EMI in year 1 is mostly interest, and in the last year is almost all principal.
    Reducing balance vs flat rate — the trap›
    A "flat rate" sounds lower but is not. On a flat rate, interest is charged on the full original amount for the whole tenure — even in the last month, when you owe almost nothing. A flat 10% is roughly equal to a reducing rate of about 18%. Always ask: "Is this reducing balance?" If the answer is not a clear yes, walk away.
    Moratorium: the interest never sleeps›
    During the course and the 6–12 months after it, education loans ask for no EMI. But interest keeps compounding on the full loan. Two quiet years can add ₹3 lakh to a ₹15 lakh loan. The fix is simple: if your family can manage it, pay the monthly interest (about ₹11,900 here) during the course. Your future self will be very grateful.
    Prepayment and part-payment›
    Paying extra — even ₹2,000 a month — cuts the loan's life by years, because every extra rupee goes straight to principal. Floating-rate home and education loans in India usually allow this free of charge; fixed-rate loans and personal loans may charge 0–2%. Always prepay early: the same ₹50,000 saves far more in year 1 than in year 8.
    Collateral vs unsecured loans›
    Loans up to ₹7.5 lakh for study in India (₹4 lakh for some banks) often need no collateral — just a co-applicant, usually a parent. Above that, banks ask for property or a fixed deposit as security, and in return the rate is usually 1–2% lower. Unsecured loans from NBFCs are faster and need no property, but cost more. Weigh the rate against the risk of pledging the family home.
    Fees nobody tells you about›
    Beyond the rate, check five things: the processing fee (0.5–1% of the loan), foreclosure charges (0–2% if you close early), late-payment penalties (usually 2% a month on the overdue EMI), insurance bundled into the loan (optional — you can say no), and documentation charges. A "low rate" with heavy fees can cost more than a slightly higher rate with none.
